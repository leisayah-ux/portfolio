### CASE STUDY 01 — Protego

**Tag:** Case Study — 01
**Context:** Protego / B2C InsurTech / UAE (RAKBank-backed)
**Title:** A 3-Year Product Vision for a Bank-Backed Digital Insurance Brand
**What this demonstrates:** Strategic Product Vision · Head of Product–level thinking
**Hero stat:** Offer extended for Head of Product role

**Summary**

A 3-year product vision and 18-month roadmap for Protego, a UAE digital insurance brand backed by RAKBank — making the case for a deliberate shift from price-aggregator to trust-led "insurance companion." Built in under a week as a Head of Product interview case study. Resulted in an offer.

**Context**

Protego is a digital insurance broker in the UAE, backed by RAKBank, operating in a market dominated by aggregators like Policybazaar and YallaCompare. The brief: define a 3-year vision and a 12–18 month roadmap. The constraint: no internal data, less than a week.

**The problem**

The aggregator model has structural limits — price competition, thin margins, low loyalty, high re-shopping costs. The real strategic question wasn't *how to be a better aggregator*. It was whether the aggregator alone was enough — or whether Protego's bank backing made something more ambitious possible.

**Approach**

I framed the strategic choice as: *what does Protego build on top of the aggregator?*

**Path A:** Stay aggregator-only. Compete on price and speed. Accept thin margins and low loyalty.
**Path B:** Build a trusted digital insurance *companion* on top of the aggregator foundation — own the relationship across compare, buy, manage, and claim.

The aggregator wasn't going away in either path — it's the entry point and a real source of customer acquisition. The question was whether Protego should *only* be that, or use it as the foundation for something defensible.

I made the case for Path B based on three advantages aggregator-only competitors can't replicate: RAKBank's distribution and trust, an ICP that values confidence over cheapest price, and the structural unwinnability of competing on scale alone against Policybazaar.

From there: a 3-year horizon (Foundations → Lifecycle Value → Ecosystem Scale) and an 18-month roadmap with phase-specific outcomes and a Month-12 PMF checkpoint.

**Key decisions and tradeoffs**

- **Companion built on top of aggregator, not instead of it.** The aggregator is a viable acquisition channel — the question is what you do with the customer once they're in. Companion features address the lifecycle gap aggregator-only models leave open.
- **Don't bet the vision on open banking.** I built embedded insurance into Year 3 but designed the companion thesis to work without it.
- **Year 1 = PMF validation, not feature delivery.** The Year 1 goal was proving customers want more than an aggregator — not shipping a feature list.

**Outcome**

Offer extended for the Head of Product role. The hire was blocked by external geopolitical events affecting Dubai-based hiring, not by fit or performance.

**What I'd do differently**

A reflection from the interview itself: both interviewers asked why Protego couldn't be both an aggregator and a companion. That was actually my position — but the way I framed Path A vs Path B made it sound like a binary choice. If I were redoing the deck, I'd reframe the fork as *"aggregator only" vs "aggregator + companion"* upfront, so the layered relationship is obvious from slide one rather than something I have to clarify in the room.

**What I'd recommend doing next, in role**

If I were stepping into this role, two priorities early on:

- **Customer interviews to validate the ICP.** I built the strategy on secondary research and reasoning. Real conversations with mass-affluent banked families would either sharpen or reshape the lens.
- **Model the unit economics.** The case for shifting from acquisition-led to retention-led growth is sharper with an LTV/CAC sketch under a few scenarios — and that's the language that lands with the board.

### CASE STUDY 02 — Embroker (Growth Function)

**Tag:** Case Study — 02
**Context:** Embroker / B2B InsurTech
**Title:** Building (and Right-Sizing) a Growth Function in a B2B InsurTech
**What this demonstrates:** Cross-functional Leadership · Organizational Judgment · Growth & Experimentation at Scale
**Hero stats:**
- +102.5% submitted applications (Law Vertical Untoggle)
- +85.3% end-to-end conversion (Email Placement)
- 83.3% experimentation velocity (vs 70% target)

**Summary**

Stood up Embroker's first dedicated growth and CRO function, building the team, the experimentation program, and the operating cadence from scratch. After a year of operating, I led the strategic decision to embed experimentation across the broader product organization rather than maintain it as a standalone discipline — adapting to organizational reality while preserving the muscle that had been built.

**Context**

Embroker is a B2B InsurTech serving small and mid-market businesses. I joined as Senior Product Manager for Growth & Innovation — now Group Product Manager — and was given a mandate by leadership to build a dedicated growth and CRO function within the broader product organization. The scope: one squad, two PM direct reports, two designers, and a content writer.

**The problem**

The company had no dedicated growth practice. Conversion optimization was happening ad hoc, experiments were rare and limited to the website, and there was no shared infrastructure or operating cadence for testing. The business goal was to use growth as a way to improve the funnel, validate hypotheses tied to the product vision, and move toward that vision through a data-driven approach.

**My role**

I owned the function end-to-end: hiring, team structure, testing infrastructure, prioritization, and operating cadence — plus the stakeholder relationships whose work intersected with the growth roadmap.

**Approach**

I built the program in four layers:

- **The team.** One squad structured around the funnel and key product surfaces, with PMs owning their own experimentation backlogs. The central function set standards and unblocked, rather than centralizing decisions.
- **Engineering capacity.** Our eng team also had to deliver on business-as-usual priorities, so I split capacity into two explicit tracks — Growth and BAU — with engineers rotating quarterly. This kept growth work from being the thing that stalled every time something unplanned hit, while still giving engineers exposure to both.
- **The infrastructure.** Established the testing stack, set hypothesis and write-up standards, built a shared experiment repository for institutional knowledge, and set a cadence for review and learning.
- **The philosophy.** With low site traffic, experimentation can't just mean A/B testing. I shaped our approach around three principles: identify where we had enough traffic to validate hypotheses through testing, evaluate Bayesian vs frequentist approaches based on the question, and pair quantitative and qualitative methods to validate hypotheses where pure A/B testing wasn't viable.

In the first year, the program drove measurable lifts across the funnel — including double-digit relative gains on submission and conversion experiments — and built a steady operating cadence of testing, learning, and iterating.

**Notable experiments originated and prioritized:**

- **Law Vertical Untoggle test** (Q4 2024 – Q1 2025): +102.5% submitted applications, +88.9% binds, +11.4% purchased premium. Confirmed "Clear Win" in Q1 2025 Growth Scorecard.
- **Email Placement test** (Q2 2025): +85.3% end-to-end conversion, +41.6% application submissions. The team's most impactful single-experiment win, validated to 100% of users.
- **"Before You Begin" Page Removal** (2024): +17% application submissions on the Law vertical, on a hypothesis that contradicted prior expectations.
- **Website UX Experiment** (Q1 2025): +17% signup conversions on the Optimal engine.

**The harder part: reading the org**

About a year in, the signals were getting harder to ignore. The organization wasn't yet positioned to support a dedicated growth function as originally scoped. Three forces were at play:

- **Business reality.** Priorities were short-term and lean; foundational work the business needed was constantly competing with experimentation work.
- **Cultural readiness.** Experimentation, hypothesis-driven thinking, and willingness to ship to learn were uneven across the org. Stakeholders frequently pushed back on *what* and *why* — not because the work was wrong, but because they didn't see it as the priority.
- **Shifting business priorities** brought a tighter focus on what the business needed in the near term.

The team was navigating between an ambitious product mandate and the near-term realities of the business. I could have defended the original mandate and pushed harder. I made a different call.

**Key decisions and tradeoffs**

The most important decision was **adapting the model rather than defending the structure.** Rather than continuing to fight for organizational space the company wasn't yet positioned to give, I led the consolidation — folding experimentation into the broader product practice rather than maintaining it as a separate discipline.

Three calls were embedded in this:

- **Adapt to the level of the org, not the ideal.** Pure growth practice — heavy testing velocity, dedicated squads, acquisition-funnel obsession — wasn't going to work here. Embedding lighter-touch experimentation into the wider product team was a more honest fit.
- **Don't burn the muscle that was built.** The PMs who had built experimentation skills kept doing it; we just stopped treating it as a standalone function with its own KPIs.
- **Communicate the why upward and downward.** Both the team and leadership needed to understand this was a strategic adaptation, not a failure of the function.

**Outcome**

What endured from the year of dedicated growth work:

- **The people.** The PMs I hired and trained continue to run experiments in their respective squads, carrying experimentation as a core part of how they operate.
- **The infrastructure.** The testing stack, hypothesis and write-up standards, and shared experiment repository remain in use across the product organization.
- **The philosophy.** Mixed-method validation — quantitative testing where traffic supported it, qualitative methods where it didn't — became part of how the broader product team approaches uncertainty.
- **The eng capacity model.** The Growth/BAU rotation pattern continued to inform how the team balances delivery work against discovery and testing.

The structural change was that experimentation stopped being a standalone function with its own KPIs and became an embedded capability across product. The principle, the people, and the practice persisted; the org chart adapted to where the company actually was.

**What I learned**

- **Organizational readiness is a real input to building a function.** Building a dedicated growth function was the right ambition for where leadership wanted to take the company, but its success depended on organizational appetite for ambiguity, long-feedback-loop bets, and a testing culture — appetite that turned out to be uneven. A sharper read on organizational readiness early on would have shaped a different operating model from day one, likely a lighter, embedded model rather than a dedicated standalone function.
- **The senior move is sometimes to right-size, not defend.** Defending the original mandate would have been easier in the short term and worse in the long term — for the team, for the company, and for the work. Being willing to scale back something I had personally built, and to communicate that clearly, was the right call.

### CASE STUDY 03 — Canadian Digital Service

**Tag:** Case Study — 03
**Context:** Canadian Digital Service / Government of Canada
**Title:** Leading at Two Scales: A National Service and a Cross-Government Consulting Squad
**What this demonstrates:** Senior Leadership · Operating Systems · Public Sector Range
**Hero stats:**
- 3 product teams led
- 6+ concurrent consulting engagements
- Decommissioning plan executed as designed

**Summary**

Led product work at the Canadian Digital Service across two simultaneous mandates: Product Lead for COVID Alert — the Government of Canada's pandemic exposure-notification service — and Consulting Team Lead for a cross-government advisory squad helping departments without internal digital capacity. The first was about leading a high-stakes service through a difficult transition. The second was about building the operating systems that let a small squad scale across the federal government.

**Context**

The Canadian Digital Service (CDS) is the Government of Canada's in-house digital team, building and supporting public-facing services. I joined as Product Lead on COVID Alert, which had been operating under sustained pressure for nearly two years. During the QR code and decommissioning phases of that work, I took on a parallel mandate as Consulting Team Lead — heading a small advisory squad partnering with federal departments that didn't have dedicated digital teams.

The two roles asked for different things: one was about leading through a high-pressure relationship environment, the other about scaling a small team's effectiveness across many engagements.

---

#### Act 1 — COVID Alert: Leading Through a Trust Rebuild

**The problem**

By the time I joined, the service had been running at crisis pace for nearly two years. The team was carrying real fatigue, and the relationship between CDS and a key public health stakeholder had eroded under cumulative pressure. There was frustration on both sides — understandable given the conditions.

A new feature roadmap wasn't going to fix this. The trust and operating-environment gaps were the root problem, and everything downstream depended on closing them.

**My role**

As Product Lead, I owned the operations of three product teams through the service's lifecycle stages, the relationship layer with internal leadership and the public health partner, and the strategic decisions about how COVID Alert evolved — including, ultimately, how it ended.

**Approach**

I leaned on three things, all core to how I work.

**Walking in without a side.** Coming in fresh was an advantage — I wasn't carrying the frustration that had built up on either side. I used that distance deliberately, refusing to default to "the CDS view" or "the partner view," and instead taking time to understand what was actually going on from each perspective.

**Empathy as a method, not a soft skill.** I treat empathy as a working approach. With stakeholders, that meant understanding what *their* goals and pressures actually were, then asking: where do their goals and mine overlap, and how can I help them get to theirs while making progress on mine? When stakeholders saw I was trying to solve *their* pain points and not just defend the team's work, the relationship started to repair on its own.

**A service lens — including the metrics.** Our own product teams were operating in silos, each focused on their piece. I started treating the service as a service: surfacing dependencies, making sure teams were talking to each other, and — critically — surfacing how we were measuring success. Even in government, performance matters. *Especially* in government. If a service isn't delivering what it's meant to deliver, the responsibility to the public is to know that, and to act on it. I pushed the team to align on what success looked like for COVID Alert and how we'd know we were getting there — because without that, you can't make honest decisions about whether to keep investing, pivot, or stop.

The empathy and the service lens together also changed how we prioritized. When you understand the full picture *and* you've named what success looks like, you stop trying to do everything. Ruthless prioritization, paradoxically, is what created the breathing room the team needed.

From there, I led the three teams through the QR code BETA work — a substantial product change requiring tight coordination with the public health partner. As the pandemic's operational profile shifted, I authored the full decommissioning strategy — covering data handling, communication, partner notification, technical sunsetting, and team transition. I left CDS before implementation, and the plan was carried out as designed.

---

#### Act 2 — The Consulting Squad: Scaling a Small Team Across Government

**The problem**

In parallel, I was leading a small consulting squad — a content designer, a designer, and two engineers — that partnered with federal departments lacking internal digital teams. Demand for our help was high, but the squad's effectiveness was limited by *how* we worked: discovery quality varied widely depending on which consultant led an engagement, and we had no way to know how many engagements we could responsibly take on at once.

**My role**

As Consulting Team Lead, I owned how the squad operated — how we framed problems, how we ran discovery, how we routed work, and how we managed capacity across more than six concurrent engagements.

**Approach**

Two operating systems made the difference.

**A problem-framing framework for discovery.** Consultants on the squad came in with different levels of experience and different approaches to product discovery — most weren't strong at problem framing, and that wasn't their core craft. I built a templatized approach to stakeholder discovery: how to enter an engagement, how to surface the real problem versus the stated one, how to structure conversations so we left every meeting with a clearer picture than we arrived with. The template was meant to be personalized by each consultant, but it standardized the output. By the end of discovery, every engagement reached a comparable, defensible understanding of the problem.

This let me empower consultants to lead engagements independently — I didn't need to be in every meeting, because the framework carried enough of the structural load.

**A capacity management pipeline.** I built a model that tracked engagements through discovery → recommendation → delivery, with realistic timeboxes for each phase. From there, I could calculate, based on the squad's actual velocity, how many engagements we could responsibly carry at each stage — and route incoming projects to consultants based on expertise and the nature of the stakeholder's problem. What had been ad hoc intake became predictable.

---

**Key decisions and tradeoffs**

Three calls were embedded across both pieces of work:

- **Don't take sides; build a third view.** On COVID Alert, the easy move would have been to align with the internal narrative. Doing the harder thing — refusing to default to either view and building my own — was what made the relationship rebuild possible.
- **Standardize the structure, personalize the application.** On the consulting squad, the discovery framework had to be tight enough to produce comparable outputs but loose enough to let each consultant lead in their own way. Getting that balance right was what made the framework actually get used.
- **Treat decommissioning as a product, not an event.** The instinct in government technology is to treat sunsets as administrative tasks. I framed decommissioning as a service design problem with users, partners, and stakeholders who all needed thoughtful handling.

**Outcome**

Across both pieces of work:

- **COVID Alert's stakeholder relationship was repaired.** Conversations became productive again. The team had the operating environment it needed to do its work.
- **The decommissioning plan was executed as designed** after my departure, winding the service down responsibly.
- **The consulting squad operated more predictably.** The discovery framework gave us a consistent way to enter engagements. The capacity model let us take on more than six concurrent projects without overcommitting.

The frameworks themselves shaped how we worked while I was there; whether they persisted afterward, I don't know.

**What I learned**

- **Empathy is a prioritization tool.** When you actually understand what each stakeholder is carrying, you stop trying to satisfy everyone equally. You make better calls about what matters, you have the courage to park what doesn't, and you create the breathing room the team needs to do good work.
- **Operating systems compound.** A well-designed framework — for discovery, for capacity, for sunsetting — does more for a team than any individual contribution. Each system I built at CDS was small in isolation, but together they changed what a small team could carry. Senior PM work, at its best, is about building the systems that let other people do their best work.

### CASE STUDY 04 — TELUS Digital

**Tag:** Case Study — 04
**Context:** TELUS Digital / B2C Telecom
**Title:** Personalizing the Renewal Journey to Drive a 10% Lift in Digital Renewal Share
**What this demonstrates:** Product Craft · Experimentation & Personalization · B2C Telecom
**Hero stats:**
- +10% digital renewal share (Year 1)
- 15 landing pages → 1 personalized experience
- First Mobility CRO function at TELUS

**Summary**

Led TELUS's first Mobility CRO team and redesigned the digital renewal journey based on how customers actually behaved, not how the business assumed they did. The work consolidated fifteen segment-specific landing pages into a single personalized experience, then extended that personalization across every touchpoint a renewing customer was likely to encounter. Result: a 10% increase in digital renewal share within the first year.

**Context**

TELUS Mobility's renewal funnel was a core business priority — renewals drive a significant share of revenue in telecom, and digital renewals are dramatically more cost-effective than agent-led ones. The team I joined was tasked with optimizing this funnel, but conversion rate optimization at TELUS Mobility was new ground: some segmentation existed, but journey-level personalization didn't.

**The problem**

When I dug into how the renewal funnel actually performed, the business's mental model didn't match customer behavior.

The assumed flow was clean: customer receives a renewal-eligible email with their offer → clicks through to a dedicated landing page → renews. The team had built around that model, maintaining roughly fifteen different segment-specific landing pages, each tied to a different offer.

The actual flow looked very different. Customers opened the email at high rates. They clicked at high rates. But they didn't renew on that visit. They came back **days or even weeks later**, through other entry points — the TELUS.com homepage, their My Account dashboard, individual phone product pages — and tried to find their offer again. And when they came back through those other paths, the offer was hard to find. It was buried in My Account, absent from the homepage, and inconsistent across the touchpoints they were actually using.

We were optimizing a one-step funnel that customers were treating as a multi-day, multi-surface journey.

**My role**

As Senior PM leading TELUS's first Mobility CRO squad — a cross-functional team of up to seven (junior POs, developers, QA, content writer, UI/UX designer) — I owned the renewal portfolio roadmap, the personalization strategy, the experimentation program, and the cross-team relationships needed to ship across surfaces I didn't directly control.

**Approach**

I built the work in three phases.

**Phase 1: Map the funnel as customers actually experienced it.** Before changing anything, I mapped the funnel end-to-end — not just the on-site behavior, but the full path from email send to renewal completion. I supplemented quantitative funnel data with qualitative input from business stakeholders, marketing, and customer service, who heard the friction firsthand. That combination is what surfaced the multi-day return-visit pattern. The funnel the business was optimizing wasn't the funnel customers were walking.

**Phase 2: Consolidate fifteen landing pages into one personalized experience.** The original landing pages were repetitive — the core content (how to renew, what the process looked like) was nearly identical across segments. Only the offer was different. Maintaining fifteen pages was a tax with no upside. I redesigned the experience into a single, highly personalized landing page: standardized structure for the renewal process, personalized offer block driven by the customer's segment. Same business outcome, dramatically simpler to maintain, and a much clearer call to action.

**Phase 3: Extend personalization across the real journey.** This was the most distinctive part of the work. Because customers were returning through multiple entry points, I designed the personalization strategy so the same offer they'd seen in the email and on the landing page followed them across the surfaces they actually used:

- **Homepage:** Identified renewal-eligible customers presented with their personalized offer and a clear call to action that deep-linked them into the renewal path.
- **My Account:** The renewal offer surfaced in highly visible placements rather than buried in account screens.
- **Product detail pages (e.g., specific phones):** A secondary promo block surfaced the same renewal offer in context, so customers comparing devices weren't pulled out of the renewal mindset.

The principle: the offer follows the customer, not the other way around.

**Key decisions and tradeoffs**

- **Map the journey before optimizing the surface.** It would have been easier to just A/B test the fifteen landing pages individually. Instead, I invested in funnel mapping and qualitative research first. That's what surfaced the cross-touchpoint pattern — and that pattern is what made the eventual lift possible.
- **Consolidation before personalization.** Going from 15 pages to one was a precondition, not a side effect. Trying to personalize 15 inconsistent pages would have made everything worse. Cleaning the foundation first made the personalization strategy executable.
- **Treat renewal as a journey, not a transaction.** The mental shift from "renewal is a single email-to-renewal funnel" to "renewal is a multi-day journey across surfaces" was the framing that changed everything.

**Outcome**

- **10% increase in digital renewal share** within the first year — a meaningful business outcome for a renewal funnel of this scale.
- **Operational simplification:** fifteen landing pages consolidated into one, reducing ongoing maintenance burden and content drift.
- **Cross-team operating change:** Personalization on the homepage and product pages required collaboration with teams that didn't report to me. The renewal use case helped establish a precedent for personalization as a cross-channel capability, not just a campaign tactic.

Beyond the renewal funnel itself, this was TELUS's first dedicated Mobility CRO function. The experimentation practice I built — hypothesis standards, test repository, evangelizing testing across other teams — became a foundation that survived and scaled past my time on the team.

**What I learned**

- **The real funnel is rarely the funnel on the slide.** Stakeholders describe how they wish the funnel worked; customers reveal how it actually works. The biggest leverage in CRO is usually closing the gap between those two pictures — not optimizing within the model the business already has.
- **Personalization is a journey strategy, not a surface tactic.** The win wasn't a better landing page. It was making sure the offer found the customer wherever they happened to be. That framing — *follow the customer across surfaces* — is the one I've carried into every CRO and growth role since.

**Summary**

Re-architected how product management work gets done at Embroker by mapping the lifecycle gaps a lean PM team faces, then designing AI-augmented workflows to close them. I test these workflows on my own work first, with the intent to scale across the team. The result is a different unit economics for PM work — strategy, design, documentation, and execution cycles that used to take days now take hours, freeing up PM capacity for the work that actually requires human judgment.

**Context**

Embroker's product organization runs lean. We don't have spare design capacity, PMs carry meaningful documentation load, and signal from customers is scattered across systems. The traditional answer to those constraints is "hire more people." The alternative — and the one I've been working on — is to redesign the workflows themselves so a lean team can operate at a higher altitude.

**The problem**

Four lifecycle gaps were limiting what we could do:

- **Limited design capacity** — design and prototyping work was bottlenecked
- **Lean PM capacity** — hours spent writing PRDs and stories crowded out strategic and vision work
- **Bug intake** — triage, prioritization, and stakeholder loop-closure were suffering under volume
- **Scattered customer feedback** — voice of customer signal lived across Salesforce, Slack, chatbots, and NPS, rarely synthesized into something usable

A new headcount request wasn't going to address any of these in the near term. What might — was rethinking the workflows themselves.

**My role**

I'm leading this work as a personal practice first, with the goal of scaling proven patterns across my team. Designing the workflows, building the agents and skills, testing them on real work, and assessing what's worth standardizing has all been mine to drive.

**Approach**

I worked from a simple principle: **identify the gap, design the workflow, test on yourself, then scale.**

I've built four workflows so far, each addressing a specific gap. They share a common logic — AI compresses the drafting, structuring, and synthesizing work, so the human time gets returned to judgment-heavy work like strategy, prioritization, and stakeholder dynamics.

**[DIAGRAM — see design instructions above]**

**The four workflows**

**1. End-to-End PM Lifecycle (proven on a real request)**
The most concrete example: a stakeholder surfaced that customers handling urgent cyber incident claims didn't know there was a specific, time-sensitive process they should be following. I worked it as a candidate for full AI-assisted execution from the start — used Figma Make in the meeting to prototype the component live, then after the meeting fed the transcript into Rovo to draft the epic and stories, refined the prototype using our design system, imported to Figma to tweak, and fed designs back to Rovo to refine the stories. PRD, epic, stories, and mockups — a few hours of work, ready for grooming in less than a day.

**2. Bug Intake Triage & Assignment Agent (partially live)**
A daily Rovo agent surfaces three views I need as a manager: new bugs submitted in the last 24 hours, tickets with new comments (so I can follow progress), and high/medium bugs in flight for more than 60 days (so aged work doesn't stall). The next phase is an auto-assignment agent that routes bugs to the right PM based on content, uses Rovo's existing duplicate detection, and pre-scores priority for PM review — with hand-off to engineering's spike/auto-build agent for straightforward fixes.

**3. Consolidated Voice of Customer (in build)**
Working with our data science team to ingest Salesforce, Slack, chatbot, and NPS feedback into a unified dashboard. The model surfaces themes across sources, quantifies frequency and severity, and lets us cross-reference with Hotjar behavioral metadata and Heap funnel data — closing the loop between *what customers say* and *what they do*. The themes feed prioritization decisions, ideation, and funnel gap analysis.

**4. AI-Augmented Product Vision (in active use)**
The workflow I built to keep strategic work from getting deprioritized. For a current vision project, I fed AI my idea, target metrics, jobs-to-be-done, and rationale, then used it as a sparring partner — refining the business case, stress-testing the idea, surfacing weaknesses I hadn't considered. **Key decisions and tradeoffs**

- **Test on myself before scaling.** Every workflow gets validated on my own work before I recommend it to the team. The cost is slower diffusion; the benefit is that I can speak to what works and what doesn't from experience, not theory.
- **AI compresses the lifecycle, not the judgment.** I'm deliberate about where AI gets to draft and where a human still has to decide. Drafts, structure, synthesis — yes. Strategic calls, prioritization, stakeholder reading — no. The point isn't to remove the PM from the loop; it's to free them to spend their time on the parts that actually require them.
- **Build for the system, not the tool.** I haven't picked a "primary AI tool" and bent every workflow to it. Each workflow uses the tool best suited to the gap — Rovo for Jira/Confluence-native work, Figma Make for design, Claude for skills and analysis. The system matters more than the brand.
- **Work within the constraints, not around them.** The way AI adoption shows up here is shaped by real organizational policies — IT security restricts certain MCP integrations, engineering doesn't want product making production changes. Rather than treating those as friction to push past, I treat them as inputs to the workflow design. The workflows I've built deliberately stop where engineering ownership begins; the AI does drafting, structuring, and synthesis, and the human-and-engineering handoffs stay where they belong. Operating well inside an organization's actual policies is part of the craft, not a constraint on it.

- **Compress the lifecycle, not the judgment.** AI is exceptionally good at drafting, structuring, and synthesizing — the parts of PM work that take hours but don't require uniquely human judgment. The point is to return that time to strategy, prioritization, and stakeholder reading. If AI starts making the calls, you've over-rotated.
- **Test on yourself before scaling.** I've watched teams roll out AI tooling top-down without proving the patterns work. The result is adoption without value. Running it on my own work first is slower diffusion, but the credibility — and the failure modes I've found — make scaling cleaner when I get there.
