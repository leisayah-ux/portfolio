## § 02 — Selected Work

### Case 01 — Embroker · B2B InsurTech
**Building (and Right-Sizing) a Growth Function**
*Headline metrics: +102.5% submitted applications · +85.3% end-to-end conversion*

**Summary.** Stood up Embroker's first dedicated growth and CRO function — the team, the experimentation program, and the operating cadence, all from scratch.

**Context & the problem.** Embroker is a B2B InsurTech serving small and mid-market businesses. I joined as Senior PM for Growth & Innovation — now GPM — with a mandate to build a dedicated growth and CRO function: one squad, two PM reports, two designers, and a content writer. There was no dedicated growth practice. Optimization happened ad hoc, experiments were rare and website-only, and there was no shared infrastructure or cadence for testing.

**Approach.** I built the program in four layers: the **team** (a squad around the funnel, PMs owning their own backlogs); **engineering capacity** (an explicit Growth/BAU split with quarterly rotation, so growth work didn't stall every time something unplanned hit); the **infrastructure** (testing stack, hypothesis and write-up standards, a shared experiment repository); and the **philosophy** (mixed-method validation for a low-traffic environment — not just A/B testing).

Selected results:
- **+102.5%** — Law Vertical Untoggle (submissions)
- **+85.3%** — Email Placement (E2E conversion)
- **+17%** — "Before You Begin" removal (submissions)
- **83.3%** — Experimentation velocity vs 70% target

**Key decision & tradeoffs.** About a year in, the signals were clear: the broader organization wasn't yet positioned to support a standalone growth function as scoped, and capacity dedicated to Experimentation was always questioned. I decided to adapt the model to the org maturity level rather than defend the structure — scale back experimentation, embed the hypothesis-driven mindset into the broader product practice, keeping the people and the muscle, and communicating the *why* both up and down.

**Outcome & what I learned.** The PMs I hired still run experiments in their squads while balancing other product-led initiatives. The testing stack, standards, and repository remain in use across the product organization. Stakeholder trust and org-wide alignment have been re-built. Organizational readiness is a real input to building a function — sometimes the right move is to right-size, not defend.

### Case 02 — Protego · UAE B2C InsurTech (RAKBank-backed)
**A 3-Year Product Vision for a Bank-Backed Digital Insurance Brand**
*Outcome: Offer extended (declined) — Head of Product role*

**Summary.** A 3-year product vision and 18-month roadmap for Protego, a UAE digital insurance brand backed by RAKBank — making the case for a deliberate shift from price-aggregator to trust-led "insurance companion." Built in under a week as a Head of Product interview case study. Resulted in an offer.

**Context & the problem.** Protego operates in a market dominated by aggregators like Policybazaar and YallaCompare. The brief: define a 3-year vision and a 12–18 month roadmap. The constraint: no internal data, less than a week. The aggregator model has structural limits — price competition, thin margins, low loyalty. The real strategic question wasn't *how to be a better aggregator*; it was whether the aggregator alone was enough, or whether Protego's bank backing made something more ambitious possible.

**Approach.** I framed the choice as: **Path A** — stay aggregator-only, compete on price, accept thin margins; or **Path B** — build a trusted digital insurance companion *on top of* the aggregator, owning the relationship across compare, buy, manage, and claim. I made the case for Path B on three advantages aggregator-only competitors can't replicate: RAKBank's distribution and trust, an ICP that values confidence over cheapest price, and the structural unwinnability of competing on scale alone. From there: a 3-year horizon (Foundations → Lifecycle Value → Ecosystem Scale) and an 18-month roadmap with a Month-12 Product Market Fit checkpoint.

**Outcome & reflection.** Offer extended for the Head of Product role. The hire was blocked by external geopolitical events affecting Dubai-based hiring — not by fit or performance.

**What I'd recommend doing next, in role.** Two priorities early on:
- Customer interviews to validate the ICP. I built the strategy on secondary research and reasoning. Real conversations with customers would either sharpen or reshape the lens.
- Model the unit economics. The case for shifting from acquisition-led to retention-led growth is sharper with an LTV/CAC sketch under a few scenarios — and that's the language that lands with the board.

### Case 03 — Canadian Digital Service · Government of Canada
**Leading at Two Scales: A National Service & a Cross-Government Consulting Squad**
*3 product teams led · 6+ concurrent engagements*

**Summary.** Led product work across two simultaneous mandates: Product Lead for COVID Alert — the Government of Canada's pandemic exposure-notification service — and Consulting Team Lead for a cross-government advisory squad helping departments without internal digital capacity. One was leading a high-stakes service through a difficult transition; the other was building the operating systems that let a small squad scale across the federal government.

**Act 1 — COVID Alert: leading through a trust rebuild.** By the time I joined, the service had run at crisis pace for nearly two years. The team carried real fatigue, and the relationship between CDS and a key public-health stakeholder had eroded. A new feature roadmap wasn't going to fix this — the trust and operating-environment gaps were the root problem. I leaned on three things: **walking in without a side** (using my distance to build a third view rather than defaulting to either narrative); **empathy as a method** (understanding each stakeholder's real goals, then finding where theirs and mine overlapped); and **a service lens — including the metrics** (naming what success looked like, because even in government, performance matters). Ruthless prioritization, paradoxically, created the breathing room the team needed. I then led the three teams through the QR-code BETA work and authored the full decommissioning strategy.

**Act 2 — the consulting squad: scaling a small team.** In parallel I led a small squad partnering with departments lacking internal digital teams. Two operating systems made the difference: a **problem-framing framework for discovery** — templatized but personalizable, so every engagement reached a comparable, defensible understanding of the problem — and a **capacity-management pipeline** that tracked engagements through discovery → recommendation → delivery, so we could responsibly carry more than six at once. Ad-hoc intake became predictable.

**Outcome & what I learned.** COVID Alert's stakeholder relationship was repaired; conversations became productive again. The decommissioning plan was executed as designed after my departure. The consulting squad operated more predictably across its engagements. Empathy is a prioritization tool — understanding what each stakeholder carries lets you make better calls about what matters. And operating systems compound: senior PM work, at its best, is building the systems that let other people do their best work.

### Case 04 — TELUS Digital · Canada B2C Telecom
**Personalizing the Renewal Journey to Drive a 10% Lift in Digital Renewal Share**
*+10% digital renewal share · 15 → 1 landing pages*

**Summary.** Led TELUS's first Mobility CRO team and redesigned the digital renewal journey based on how customers actually behaved, not how the business assumed they did — consolidating fifteen segment-specific landing pages into a single personalized experience, then extending that personalization across every touchpoint a renewing customer encounters. Result: a 10% increase in digital renewal share within the first year.

**The problem.** The business's mental model didn't match customer behavior. The assumed flow was clean: email → dedicated landing page → renew. The actual flow was a multi-day, multi-surface journey — customers opened and clicked at high rates but came back days or weeks later through the homepage, My Account, and product pages, where the offer was buried or absent. We were optimizing a one-step funnel that customers were treating as a journey.

*(Figure: the renewal journey mapped end-to-end, with every touchpoint where the offer was absent marked — customers return days later through paths the funnel never accounted for.)*

**Approach.**
- **Phase 1 — map the real funnel.** I mapped the path end-to-end and paired quantitative data with qualitative input from stakeholders, marketing, and customer service. That's what surfaced the multi-day return-visit pattern.
- **Phase 2 — consolidate fifteen pages into one.** The pages were near-identical except for the offer. I redesigned them into a single personalized experience: standardized renewal structure, personalized offer block driven by segment.
- **Phase 3 — extend personalization across the journey.** The same offer followed the customer across the homepage, My Account, and product detail pages. The principle: the offer follows the customer, not the other way around.

*(Figure: the redesign — the same personalized offer surfaced across the homepage, device catalogue, product pages, and plan configuration, so it followed the customer wherever they returned.)*

**Outcome & what I learned.** A 10% increase in digital renewal share within the first year; fifteen landing pages consolidated into one; and a cross-team precedent for personalization as a capability, not a campaign tactic. The experimentation practice I built became a foundation that scaled past my time on the team. The real funnel is rarely the funnel on the slide — the biggest leverage in CRO is closing the gap between how the business wishes it worked and how customers actually behave. And personalization is a journey strategy, not a surface tactic.

### Case 05 — Embroker · AI Ops · Modern Craft
**Designing an AI-Augmented PM Operating Model**
*4 workflows designed & deployed · days → hours on the first proven workflow*

**Summary.** Re-architected how PM work gets done at Embroker by mapping the lifecycle gaps a lean PM team faces, then designing AI-augmented workflows to close them. I test each workflow on my own work first, with the intent to scale across the team. The result is different unit economics for PM work — strategy, design, documentation, and execution cycles that used to take days now take hours.

**The problem.** Four lifecycle gaps were limiting a lean org: limited design capacity, lean PM capacity, bug intake suffering under volume, and customer feedback scattered across Salesforce, Slack, chatbots, and NPS. A headcount request wasn't going to address any of them in the near term and was not an option — rethinking the workflows might.

**Approach — identify the gap, design the workflow, test on yourself, then scale.** Four workflows so far, each addressing a specific gap. They share a logic: AI compresses the drafting, structuring, and synthesizing — so human time gets returned to judgment-heavy work like strategy, prioritization, and stakeholder dynamics.

The most concrete: a stakeholder surfaced that customers handling urgent cyber-incident claims didn't know there was a time-sensitive process to follow. I prototyped the component live in the meeting with Figma Make, fed the transcript into Rovo to draft the epic and stories, refined the prototype against our design system, and looped designs back to refine the stories. PRD, epic, stories, and mockups — a few hours of work, ready for grooming in under a day.

**Outcome & what I learned.** It's early, and the honest framing is: I've proven the patterns on my own work, presented to PMs who are currently testing them. The cyber-incident workflow turned a multi-day cycle into a few hours; it also created a different relationship with stakeholders, who are now active participants in shaping the solution. The triage agent gives me daily oversight I used to assemble by hand; the vision workflow is keeping a strategic project alive that would otherwise have been deprioritized. The next phase is gathering feedback and tweaking the workflows, scaling these patterns into a team operating model.

*Stack: Figma Make · Claude (chat, code, design) · ChatGPT · Perplexity · Gemini · Rovo · Lovable.*

**The operating model — four workflows.** Each card closes a specific PM lifecycle gap. The point isn't tool adoption — it's redesigning how PM work gets done so capacity gets returned to the work that requires human judgment.

1. **End-to-End PM Lifecycle** *(Design + Execution · Live)* — Gap: lean PM capacity, limited design support; contained requests take a week to document and design.
   1. Stakeholder meeting → prototype the component live with Figma Make
   2. Feed meeting transcript to Rovo → draft epic and stories
   3. Refine prototype in Figma Make using our design system → variants per touchpoint
   4. Import to Figma, tweak → feed designs back to Rovo to refine stories
   5. Assess fit for engineering's auto-build agent for expedited execution
   *Impact: shorten intake and delivery cycle from days to hours.*

2. **Triage & Assignment Agent** *(Bug Intake · Partial)* — Gap: lean team struggles to triage bugs, prioritize, and close the loop with stakeholders.
   1. Daily Rovo agent surfaces: new bugs, tickets with new comments, aged high/medium bugs in flight
   2. *(In progress)* Auto-assignment agent: routes bugs to the right PM
   3. *(In progress)* Duplicate detection via Rovo + priority pre-scoring (PM reviews)
   4. *(Planned)* Hand-off to engineering's spike/auto-build agent for straightforward fixes
   *Status: triage agent live; assignment + duplicate detection in build.*

3. **Consolidated Feedback Synthesis** *(Voice of Customer · In build)* — Gap: customer signal lives across Salesforce, Slack, chatbots, and NPS — rarely synthesized into something usable.
   1. Partner with data science to ingest all VoC channels into a unified dashboard
   2. Model surfaces themes across sources → quantifies frequency and severity
   3. Cross-reference with Hotjar behavioral metadata + Heap funnel data
   4. Themes feed prioritization, ideation, and funnel gap analysis
   *Status: dashboard in build with data science partnership.*

4. **AI-Augmented Product Vision** *(Strategy + Vision · Live)* — Gap: strategic and vision work gets deprioritized when PM capacity is tight.
   1. Use AI as a sparring partner: refine business case, stress-test the idea, surface weaknesses
   2. Benchmark via custom competitive analysis skill (Claude, biweekly report)
   3. Generate design variations with Claude Design → select direction
   4. Refine into prototype with Claude Code → share with engineering and stakeholders
   *Status: actively used for a current vision project.*

## § 04 — How I Work

1. **Product work starts with the problem, not the solution.** Before anything ships, I push the team to align on three things: what problem we're actually solving, what success looks like through both a user lens and a business lens, and how we'll know the solution moved the outcome we cared about. At the GPM level, this matters more than feature velocity — because the roadmap directly shapes P&L. Prioritization and sequencing decisions feed revenue, retention, and cost. Treating the roadmap as a delivery plan rather than a financial decision is one of the most common gaps I see in product work.

2. **I read the org before I optimize the product.** Most PM work happens inside organizational reality — capacity, culture, politics, priorities. I treat that reality as an input to strategy, not friction to push past. The right roadmap inside the wrong operating environment is still the wrong work.

3. **I build systems, not just outputs.** A feature ships once. A framework, an operating model, an experimentation program — those compound. The most leveraged PM work I've done has been building the structures that let other people do their best work, and I think about every role through that lens.

4. **Empathy is a prioritization tool, not a soft skill.** When I actually understand what each stakeholder is carrying — what they're measured on, what's making their job hard — I make better calls about what matters and what can wait. Empathy isn't kindness; it's a way of building the third view that lets you decide well.

5. **AI changes the unit economics of PM work, not the judgment.** I use AI to compress drafting, structuring, and synthesis — and to keep PM capacity returned to the work that requires human judgment: strategy, prioritization, and stakeholder reading. If AI starts making the calls, you've over-rotated.
